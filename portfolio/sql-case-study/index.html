SQL Case Study

Diagnosing a quarterly margin decline at Ardwell Consumer Goods

A worked SQL analysis on a fictional FMCG dataset, escalating from basic aggregation to a price, volume and cost bridge that pinpoints exactly which product lines moved margin, and why.

SQLite Star schema CTEs Window functions PVM decomposition

The brief

Ardwell Consumer Goods is a fictional FMCG business spanning beverages, snacks, household and personal care lines, sold through grocery, convenience, wholesale and online channels across four Australian metros. The finance team flagged that gross margin slipped in the third quarter and wanted to know where it came from before the quarterly review.

Which product lines drove the Q3 margin decline, and was it volume, price or cost?

The dataset holds roughly 30,000 sales rows across the 2025 calendar year. Every query below was run against it and the result tables show the actual output.

The data model

A single sales fact table at transaction grain, surrounded by four dimensions. The same star schema discipline I apply when building semantic models in Power BI: one fact, conformed dimensions, no snowflaking.

Gross margin is defined as net revenue minus COGS. Net revenue is gross revenue less trade and promotional discount.

The analysis

Five steps, each one a layer deeper. The early queries establish where margin lives and confirm the decline is real. The later ones isolate the lines responsible and break the movement into its drivers.

Step 01 · Aggregation

Where does margin sit across the range?

GROUP BY · derived margin % 
SELECT
    p.category,
    ROUND(SUM(f.net_revenue), 0)                          AS net_revenue,
    ROUND(SUM(f.net_revenue) - SUM(f.cogs), 0)            AS gross_margin,
    ROUND(100.0 * (SUM(f.net_revenue) - SUM(f.cogs))
                 / SUM(f.net_revenue), 1)                 AS margin_pct
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_id = f.product_id
GROUP BY p.category
ORDER BY gross_margin DESC;

So what: Personal Care earns the richest rate (56%) on the smallest revenue base, while Snacks is the volume engine but the thinnest margin at 41.4%. Knowing where the rate is fattest tells you which lines a few points of slippage will hurt most.

Step 02 · Joins

Is the slip concentrated in one channel?

multi-table join · channel × quarter grain 
SELECT
    c.channel,
    d.quarter,
    ROUND(SUM(f.net_revenue), 0)                          AS net_revenue,
    ROUND(100.0 * (SUM(f.net_revenue) - SUM(f.cogs))
                 / SUM(f.net_revenue), 1)                 AS margin_pct
FROM fact_sales f
JOIN dim_customer c ON c.customer_id = f.customer_id
JOIN dim_date d     ON d.date_id     = f.date_id
GROUP BY c.channel, d.quarter
ORDER BY c.channel, d.quarter;

Result · margin % shown pivoted for readability

channelQ1Q2Q3Q4
Grocery48.3%48.3%45.1%48.2%
Convenience48.3%48.2%45.0%48.2%
Wholesale48.3%48.2%45.1%48.3%
Online48.3%48.3%45.0%48.3%

So what: Every channel dips by almost exactly the same amount in Q3 and recovers in Q4. A uniform fall rules out a channel-specific cause like one retailer's trade deal. The problem travels with the products, not the route to market, so the next cut goes by product.

Step 03 · CTE

How big is the decline, and is it structural?

WITH clause · quarter-on-quarter 
WITH quarterly AS (
    SELECT
        d.quarter,
        SUM(f.net_revenue)                AS net_revenue,
        SUM(f.net_revenue) - SUM(f.cogs)  AS gross_margin
    FROM fact_sales f
    JOIN dim_date d ON d.date_id = f.date_id
    GROUP BY d.quarter
)
SELECT
    quarter,
    ROUND(net_revenue, 0)                         AS net_revenue,
    ROUND(gross_margin, 0)                        AS gross_margin,
    ROUND(100.0 * gross_margin / net_revenue, 1)  AS margin_pct
FROM quarterly
ORDER BY quarter;

Result

quarternet_revenuegross_marginmargin_pct
Q1$631,583$304,86648.3%
Q2$642,554$310,22348.3%
Q3$635,511$286,40945.1%
Q4$650,893$314,11548.3%

So what: Margin rate fell 3.2 points in Q3, around $24k of gross margin lost against Q2 on broadly flat revenue, then snapped back in Q4. This is a contained, single-quarter event rather than a structural decline, which points to a handful of lines with a temporary problem.

Step 04 · Window functions

Which lines own the gap?

conditional pivot · RANK() OVER 
WITH prod_q AS (
    SELECT
        p.product_name,
        d.quarter,
        SUM(f.net_revenue) - SUM(f.cogs)                  AS gross_margin,
        100.0 * (SUM(f.net_revenue) - SUM(f.cogs))
              / SUM(f.net_revenue)                        AS margin_pct
    FROM fact_sales f
    JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_id = f.product_id
    JOIN dim_date d    ON d.date_id    = f.date_id
    WHERE d.quarter IN ('Q2','Q3')
    GROUP BY p.product_name, d.quarter
),
pivoted AS (
    SELECT
        product_name,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q2' THEN margin_pct   END) AS q2_margin_pct,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q3' THEN margin_pct   END) AS q3_margin_pct,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q2' THEN gross_margin END) AS q2_margin,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q3' THEN gross_margin END) AS q3_margin
    FROM prod_q
    GROUP BY product_name
)
SELECT
    product_name,
    ROUND(q2_margin_pct, 1)                       AS q2_margin_pct,
    ROUND(q3_margin_pct, 1)                       AS q3_margin_pct,
    ROUND(q3_margin_pct - q2_margin_pct, 1)       AS pct_pts_change,
    ROUND(q3_margin - q2_margin, 0)               AS margin_change,
    RANK() OVER (ORDER BY q3_margin - q2_margin)  AS decline_rank
FROM pivoted
ORDER BY margin_change
LIMIT 6;

Step 05 · Price–Volume–Cost bridge

Volume, price or cost?

bridge logic · per-unit economics 
WITH base AS (
    SELECT
        p.product_name, d.quarter,
        SUM(f.units_sold)   AS units,
        SUM(f.net_revenue)  AS net_rev,
        SUM(f.cogs)         AS cogs
    FROM fact_sales f
    JOIN dim_product p ON p.product_id = f.product_id
    JOIN dim_date d    ON d.date_id    = f.date_id
    WHERE d.quarter IN ('Q2','Q3')
    GROUP BY p.product_name, d.quarter
),
metrics AS (
    SELECT
        product_name,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q2' THEN units END)          AS q2_units,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q3' THEN units END)          AS q3_units,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q2' THEN net_rev/units END)  AS q2_price,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q3' THEN net_rev/units END)  AS q3_price,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q2' THEN cogs/units END)     AS q2_cost,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q3' THEN cogs/units END)     AS q3_cost,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q2' THEN net_rev - cogs END) AS q2_margin,
        MAX(CASE WHEN quarter='Q3' THEN net_rev - cogs END) AS q3_margin
    FROM base GROUP BY product_name
)
SELECT
    product_name,
    ROUND((q3_units - q2_units) * (q2_price - q2_cost), 0) AS volume_effect,
    ROUND((q3_price - q2_price) * q3_units, 0)             AS price_effect,
    ROUND(-(q3_cost  - q2_cost)  * q3_units, 0)            AS cost_effect,
    ROUND(q3_margin - q2_margin, 0)                       AS total_change
FROM metrics
ORDER BY total_change
LIMIT 3;

Result

product_namevolumepricecosttotal Δ
Cold Brew Coffee 750ml+$997$0-$14,222-$13,224
Laundry Liquid 2L-$11,055$0$0-$11,055
Dark Chocolate Bar 100g+$5,376-$12,459$0-$7,083

So what: The bridge answers the brief line by line. Cold Brew is a cost shock, a 34% jump in bean cost stripping $14k, recoverable with a price rise or a supply hedge. Laundry Liquid is pure volume, $11k lost to a competitor launch with rate untouched, so it is a demand problem not a margin one. Dark Chocolate is self-inflicted, $12.5k of price erosion from promo depth that the extra $5.4k of volume only partly paid back. Three lines, three different fixes, owned by three different teams.

What this demonstrates

  • Star schema modelling. One fact table at transaction grain with conformed dimensions, the same structure I build semantic models on in Power BI.
  • Layered SQL. Aggregation and joins, CTEs to keep multi-step logic readable, and window functions with conditional pivots to compare periods side by side.
  • A price–volume–cost bridge. Decomposing a margin movement into its drivers is standard commercial finance work, and writing it in SQL means it runs on the warehouse rather than in a spreadsheet someone has to maintain.
  • Commercial reading. Every query closes with what the number means for the business and what to do next, which is the part that turns a query result into a decision.

Built on a fictional dataset of ~30,000 rows in SQLite. The same queries run unchanged on BigQuery, Databricks or Fabric warehouse with only minor dialect tweaks.
